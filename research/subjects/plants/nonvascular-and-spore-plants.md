# Nonvascular and spore-bearing plants

These profiles extend the catalogue beyond flowering plants. Mosses are bryophytes with a dominant gametophyte; lycophytes, ferns and horsetails are vascular plants that reproduce by spores. They must not be drawn using the same leaf, root, flower or branching assumptions as angiosperms.

## Common haircap moss — *Polytrichum commune*

- **Architecture:** erect, wiry, usually simple leafy gametophores form dense turfs; rhizoids anchor the shoots.
- **Leaves:** small, spirally arranged, narrow-lanceolate leaves with a clasping sheath, toothed blade and strong costa. The upper surface bears photosynthetic lamellae. Leaves spread when moist and become more erect or twisted when dry.
- **Reproduction:** no flowers or seeds. Separate male and female reproductive shoots; the female shoot may carry a long reddish seta and an angular capsule.
- **Important distinction:** the conspicuous green shoot is the gametophyte; the capsule-bearing sporophyte is attached to it.
- **References:** SANBI, [*Polytrichum commune*](https://pza.sanbi.org/polytrichum-commune), supports shoot habit, leaf arrangement, lamellae and sporophyte; Oxford Plants 400, [*Polytrichum commune*](https://herbaria.plants.ox.ac.uk/bol/plants400/Profiles/op/Polytrichum), supports life cycle and capsule morphology.

## Peat moss — *Sphagnum* spp. (genus-level profile)

- **Architecture:** upright stems with fascicles of spreading and pendent branches; crowded upper branches form a capitulum.
- **Leaves:** small overlapping leaves contain large, empty hyaline water-storage cells interspersed with narrow living photosynthetic cells.
- **Reproduction:** no flowers or seeds; capsules release spores. In some species the operculum is expelled as the capsule dries.
- **Scope warning:** this is a genus-level structural profile, not a species-level substitute. Species differ in branch arrangement, capitulum shape, leaf anatomy and capsule details.
- **References:** University of Auckland, [Sphagnum species](https://www.nzplants.auckland.ac.nz/en/about/mosses/native-species/sphagnaceae/sphagnum-species.html), supports stem, branch and leaf-cell structure; American Journal of Botany, [Polarity of peatmoss evolution](https://doi.org/10.3732/ajb.90.12.1777), supports fasciculate dimorphic branching and capitula.

## Running clubmoss — *Lycopodium clavatum*

- **Lineage traits:** lycophytes are vascular spore plants with true roots, stems and microphylls; each microphyll has one vein.
- **Reproduction:** sporangia occur on sporophylls; terminal aggregations of sporophylls form strobili.
- **Evidence limit:** the cited teaching references establish lycophyte morphology and the genus-level clubmoss structure. A dedicated species flora is still needed before exact *L. clavatum* shoot shape, leaf margins and strobilus proportions are marked fully verified.
- **References:** University of Wisconsin Botany 130, [Lycophytes](https://grahamlab.botany.wisc.edu/botany_130/diversity/plants/Vascular_Plants/Lycophyta/Objectives.html); SDSU Plant Systematics Laboratory Manual, [Lycopodiophyta](https://plants.sdsu.edu/plantsystematics/labs/plsyslab.pdf).

## Ferns — bracken and northern maidenhair

- **Shared lineage traits:** true fern sporophytes bear fronds; sporangia are commonly grouped into sori, often on frond undersides. The gametophyte is a small prothallus.
- **Bracken, *Pteridium aquilinum*:** catalogue profile records the large, divided frond and creeping rhizome as a provisional species construction; the cited fern reference supports sori and fern life cycle generally, not all bracken-specific characters.
- **Northern maidenhair, *Adiantum pedatum*:** catalogue profile records a fanlike, forked frond architecture as a provisional species construction; species-specific frond and soral details still require a flora or botanical-garden account.
- **References:** UBC Botanical Garden, [Ferns](https://botanicalgarden.ubc.ca/our-collections/ferns/), supports fern sori and gametophyte life cycle. Species-specific records remain explicitly provisional until more exact sources are attached.

## Field horsetail — *Equisetum arvense*

- **Architecture:** rhizomatous perennial with two distinct shoot forms. Early fertile shoots are short, brown, unbranched and topped by a strobilus; later sterile shoots are green, jointed and bear whorls of slender branches.
- **Leaves:** greatly reduced scale leaves are fused into sheaths at the nodes; the green lateral branches provide most photosynthesis.
- **Reproduction:** no flowers or seeds; spores form in the terminal strobilus of fertile shoots.
- **References:** USDA Forest Service FEIS, [*Equisetum arvense*](https://research.fs.usda.gov/feis/species-reviews/equarv), supports dimorphic shoots and stem anatomy; Utah State University Extension, [Horsetail](https://extension.usu.edu/rangeplants/forbs-herbaceous/horsetail), supports rhizomes, whorled branches and reduced leaves.

## Kentucky bluegrass — *Poa pratensis*

- **Architecture:** cool-season perennial grass, erect flowering culms, fibrous roots and creeping rhizomes that form sod.
- **Leaves:** linear parallel-veined blades; the sheath wraps the culm and a membranous blunt ligule occurs at the blade-sheath junction. The leaf tip is boat-shaped.
- **Reproduction:** small florets are grouped in spikelets within a terminal open panicle; fruit is a caryopsis.
- **References:** Kew POWO, [Poaceae morphology](https://powo.science.kew.org/taxon/urn%3Alsid%3Aipni.org%3Anames%3A30000032-2/general-information), supports family-level culm, leaf sheath, ligule and blade structure; Penn State Extension, [Kentucky bluegrass](https://plantscience.psu.edu/outreach/plant-id/grasses/kentucky-bluegrass), supports *P. pratensis* rhizomes and ligule; Clemson Extension, [Perennial grasses](https://www.clemson.edu/cafls/research/weeds/weed-id-bio/grasses/perennial-grass.html), supports growth habit and panicle/spikelet traits.

## Construction rules

- Never draw mosses as miniature flowering plants: the leafy gametophyte and attached capsule-bearing sporophyte are distinct structures.
- Never give lycophytes or horsetails flowers, fruits or angiosperm leaves.
- Fern fronds are compound organs; their repeated divisions belong to the frond, not to a recursively branching woody stem.
- Grass blades attach through sheaths to jointed culms; a grass inflorescence is made of spikelets, not daisy-like flowers.
- Where species-level evidence is incomplete, preserve the gap instead of promoting family-level traits to verified species facts.
